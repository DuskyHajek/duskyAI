Concepts

Don’t mix up

The exam often asks which of two or three similar ideas fits a scenario. Cues below are study heuristics, not official AWS definitions.

Prompt engineering vs retrieval augmented generation vs fine-tuning

D1 · Fundamentals
DimensionPrompt engineeringRAGFine-tuning
What it isImproves instructions, context, and examples sent to the model.Fetches current company content at question time and adds it to the prompt.Further trains the model on your examples so its behavior shifts.
Pick it whenOutput format, tone, or constraints are underspecified.Facts are private, change often, or need source citations.You need a consistent style or task behavior prompts cannot reach.

On the exam, start with the cheapest change that fixes the problem. Unclear output points to the prompt, missing or weekly-changing facts point to retrieval, and a consistent style or behavior the prompt cannot reach points to fine-tuning.

Quick check An internal FAQ bot quotes last month’s travel policy. What do you try first?

Use retrieval augmented generation (RAG) over the live policy library so answers pull current passages with citations, rather than fine-tuning on documents that keep changing.

Artificial intelligence vs machine learning vs generative AI

D1 · Fundamentals
DimensionArtificial intelligenceMachine learningGenerative AI
What it isThe broad umbrella for machines performing tasks that normally need human intelligence.Models that learn patterns from historical data to predict, score, or classify.Models that create new text, images, audio, or code from a prompt.
Pick it whenReferring to the overall field or portfolio.Scoring churn, forecasting demand, or routing tickets from labeled history.Drafting copy, summarizing calls, or synthesizing unstructured text.

On the exam, scoring or predicting from past data is machine learning, creating new content is generative AI, and fixed if-then logic is neither.

Quick check A retailer wants to predict which subscribers will cancel next quarter from billing history. Which category fits?

Machine learning (predictive AI), because the goal is scoring a structured outcome from historical patterns, not generating new content.

Rule-based automation vs AI

D1 · Fundamentals

Rule-based automation follows fixed logic written by people and never learns. AI handles cases too varied or unstructured to write rules for.

On the exam, if the logic is simple, stable, and must be auditable, rules beat AI. If the inputs are messy or the patterns change, AI earns its cost.

Quick check A regulator publishes exact numeric thresholds for flagging loans, and auditors require 100% reproducible outcomes. AI or rules?

Rule-based automation. The criteria are explicit, structured, stable, and must be deterministic.

AWS Quick vs Bedrock vs SageMaker AI

D1 · Fundamentals
DimensionAWS QuickAmazon BedrockAmazon SageMaker AI
What it isReady-made AI workspace for business users (the exam guide still calls it Amazon Quick).Managed platform for building generative AI apps on foundation models, with retrieval and guardrails.End-to-end platform where a team builds, trains, and runs its own custom models.
Pick it whenNon-technical staff need analysis, research, or workflow automation with no builders.Developers are integrating foundation models into a company app.Off-the-shelf foundation models will not fit and custom ML training is required.

On the exam, business users with no builders point to Quick. A developer building a generative AI app points to Bedrock. A team that needs its own model points to SageMaker AI.

Quick check A finance team with no engineers wants an assistant over internal spreadsheets and docs this month. Where do you point them?

AWS Quick (Amazon Quick), because business users without engineering builders need a ready-made workspace.

AI agent vs chatbot

D1 · Fundamentals

A chatbot replies. An AI agent plans steps, uses business tools, checks the result, and acts or escalates.

On the exam, a system that must take actions across tools is an agent. One that only answers questions is an assistant.

Quick check A travel tool must look up flight rules, rebook a ticket, file an expense claim, and email the manager. Chatbot or agent?

AI agent, because it plans multi-step work and executes actions across external systems rather than only answering questions.

Augmentation vs automation

D1 · Fundamentals

Augmentation keeps a person doing the job, with AI as the draft or recommendation. Automation lets the system carry out the job.

On the exam, a person still producing the outcome is augmentation. Whether each case must be approved is a separate human-in-the-loop question.

Quick check AI drafts customer replies that support reps edit and send themselves. Augmentation or automation?

Augmentation, because the person remains responsible for producing and sending the final outcome.

Token vs context window

D1 · Fundamentals

A token is the unit of text a model reads and writes, and usage is typically priced by it. A context window is how much text the model can consider in one request.

On the exam, cost that climbs with long prompts is a token problem. Details lost from very long inputs or long chats point to context limits — the fix is to send only relevant passages, such as with retrieval, not the whole library.

Quick check Short leases summarize accurately, but 120-page leases silently drop clauses from the end with no error logged. What is the cause?

The input exceeded the context window. Retrieve and pass only relevant sections instead of stuffing the entire document into one prompt.

KPI vs ROI vs baseline

D2 · Strategy
DimensionKPIROIBaseline
What it isThe metric the initiative exists to move.Realized benefits minus full costs, divided by costs.The measurement taken before AI so later change can be credited to it.
Pick it whenDefining what operational or customer success looks like.Justifying spend against total cost of ownership.Proving a before-and-after lift rather than guessing from memory.

On the exam, a number with no before-measurement is not yet a result, and saved minutes are not ROI until converted into realized cost, capacity, or revenue.

Quick check Six months after launch, a team reports 4-minute average handling time and asks to declare success, but nobody recorded pre-AI times. What is missing?

A baseline. Without a before-measurement (or controlled comparison), the 4-minute figure cannot prove AI caused an improvement.

Impact vs feasibility

D2 · Strategy

Business impact is the value if the use case works. Feasibility is whether the data, skills, risk, and time allow it to work.

On the exam, the first use case scores high on both. High impact with weak data is a data project first, not a pilot.

Quick check Use case A promises $5M upside but lacks clean historical records; use case B promises $1M upside with ready data and a clear owner. Which runs first?

Use case B. High impact with weak feasibility stalls; pair A with a data-readiness track before piloting it.

Build vs buy vs partner

D2 · Strategy
DimensionBuildBuyPartner
What it isCreating a custom solution with your own team.Adopting a ready-made product as-is.Working with a vendor, integrator, or AWS Marketplace offering to supply skills or a tailored solution.
Pick it whenAI is your differentiator, constraints are strict, and you have the skills.The need is common and speed matters more than customization.You need delivery speed or specialized capability your team lacks.

On the exam, if AI is your differentiator and you have the skills, build. For common needs, buy. When you lack skills or time, partner — and always check hard legal or accuracy constraints first.

Quick check A mid-sized firm needs standard invoice extraction in 60 days and has no ML engineers. Build, buy, or partner?

Buy (or partner for integration). Invoice extraction is a common need and the short deadline plus lack of ML staff rules out building from scratch.

Consumption-based vs seat-based vs instance-based pricing

D2 · Strategy
DimensionConsumption-basedSeat-basedInstance-based
What it isPay per unit used, such as tokens or API calls.Pay a recurring fee per named user.Pay for dedicated compute or reserved capacity while it runs.
Pick it whenDemand is variable, spiky, or still being tested.Employees use a packaged workspace regularly and budgets need predictability.Workloads run at high, steady volume where reserved capacity or Savings Plans lower unit cost.

On the exam, pay-per-use fits variable or experimental demand, while seats or reserved capacity fit steady, predictable use — avoid long commitments before usage stabilizes.

Quick check A marketing team plans a 6-week pilot with unpredictable traffic and asks whether to commit to reserved capacity upfront. What should you advise?

Start with consumption-based pricing. Reserve capacity or Savings Plans only after demand proves steady and ongoing.

Hallucination vs bias vs data-quality degradation

D3 · Governance
DimensionHallucinationBiasData-quality degradation
What it isFluent output that is false or unsupported by any source.Results that are systematically worse for certain groups or cases.Inputs become stale, incomplete, or inconsistent, causing performance to decay (drift).
Pick it whenThe model invents citations, numbers, or policy details.Historical labels or proxies skew outcomes against a demographic or segment.A system worked well at launch and gradually grew less accurate as inputs shifted.

On the exam, confident wrong facts point to hallucination. Consistently worse outcomes for one group point to bias. A system that worked at launch and slowly got worse points to data-quality degradation or drift.

Quick check A demand model launched with strong accuracy, but six months later forecasts miss after two new product lines and missing warehouse feeds. Which issue is this?

Data-quality degradation and drift — the model code did not change, but the inputs and underlying distribution shifted over time.

Guardrails vs human oversight vs governance

D3 · Governance
DimensionGuardrailsHuman oversightGovernance
What it isAutomated runtime controls that block or filter unsafe inputs and outputs.A person reviewing, approving (HITL), or overriding (HOTL) decisions.Policies, roles, and accountability that decide which controls apply.
Pick it whenPreventing blocked topics, PII leaks, or ungrounded replies in real time.Errors carry meaningful harm or low-confidence cases need judgment.Setting standards, approval gates, inventory, and risk ownership across teams.

On the exam, an automatic block on a harmful answer is a guardrail. A person approving or stopping a decision is oversight. Deciding who owns the risk and which controls apply is governance.

Quick check Three departments deploy chatbots with different privacy rules and no central review. Adding a prompt filter is not enough — what layer is missing?

Governance: shared policy, an AI inventory, approval gates, and named owners deciding which controls each use case requires.

Human-in-the-loop vs human-on-the-loop

D3 · Governance

Human-in-the-loop means a person approves each decision before it counts. Human-on-the-loop means the system acts and a person monitors and can override it.

On the exam, a high-harm or low-confidence case is escalated to a person. A high-harm decision that must not go out unreviewed is human-in-the-loop. A system that acts, with someone able to stop it, is human-on-the-loop.

Quick check An AI flags suspicious card transactions and blocks them automatically while fraud analysts monitor alerts and reverse false positives. Which model is this?

Human-on-the-loop, because the system executes actions without waiting for per-case sign-off, while people watch and can intervene.

Explainability vs transparency

D3 · Governance

Explainability is being able to say why the system produced an output. Transparency is telling people that AI is involved and what it can and cannot do.

On the exam, a regulator asking why a decision was made is explainability. A customer who should know they are talking to AI is transparency.

Quick check A bank must tell declined credit applicants which factors drove their rejection. Explainability or transparency?

Explainability, because the bank must account for why that specific decision happened.

ISO/IEC 22989 vs 23053 vs 42001

D3 · Governance
DimensionISO/IEC 22989ISO/IEC 23053ISO/IEC 42001
What it isShared AI concepts and terminology dictionary.Framework describing the parts of a machine-learning system.Certifiable AI management system standard for an organization.
Pick it whenTeams and vendors need one common vocabulary.Mapping ML system components (not for company certification).Auditing or certifying how an organization manages AI risk and controls.

On the exam, a vendor’s 42001 certificate does not certify its customers, and 23053 is not a certificate at all.

Quick check A vendor claims its ISO/IEC 42001 certificate means your deployed AI workflow is already certified. True or false?

False. A vendor’s ISO/IEC 42001 certificate covers the vendor’s management system, not how your organization governs its own AI use cases.

Proof of concept vs pilot vs scale

D4 · Readiness
DimensionProof of conceptPilotScale
What it isSmall, time-boxed test of whether an approach can work at all.Controlled trial with real users, real data, and measured outcomes.Enterprise rollout with ownership, monitoring, support, and operating routines.
Pick it whenValidating technical feasibility before spending on workflow rollout.Proving real-world value and adoption before full investment.Moving a proven pilot into repeatable production across teams.

On the exam, a successful demo is not production readiness. Many pilots with no owner or operating plan is pilot purgatory.

Quick check Five teams ran impressive AI demos last year, yet none reached daily operations because no one owns monitoring or support. What stage failed?

Scaling (pilot purgatory). Demos and pilots succeeded, but run-state ownership, support, and operational governance were never built.

Accountable owner vs center of excellence

D4 · Readiness

The accountable owner answers for one AI system’s outcomes and risks. The AI center of excellence provides standards, reusable assets, and enablement across the company.

On the exam, who answers for a failure is the owner. Who helps every team do it well is the center of excellence.

Quick check A central AI team insists on building and owning every business unit’s models, creating a 9-month backlog. What is wrong with that operating model?

The center of excellence became a delivery bottleneck. It should enable teams with standards and reusable patterns while business owners stay accountable for outcomes.

AI literacy vs upskilling vs change management

D4 · Readiness
DimensionAI literacyUpskillingChange management
What it isThe practical skill to use, question, and govern AI in one’s role.Role-based training programs that build those skills across staff.Aligning communication, workflows, incentives, and leadership so people adopt new ways of working.
Pick it whenDefining the baseline capability every role needs.Closing a knowledge or tool-usage gap through targeted training.Staff understand the tool yet keep using the old process because of habits, fear, or misaligned incentives.

On the exam, a working tool that few people use points to change management, not a better model.

Quick check Dispatchers completed AI training on a route optimizer but still plan routes manually because bonuses reward manual overrides. What is needed?

Change management — specifically realigning incentives, manager signals, and workflow expectations rather than adding more technical training.

D3 · Risk classification

EU AI Act four risk tiers

Obligations scale with potential harm rather than applying one blanket rule to every AI use case.

  1. Tier 1 · Highest

    Prohibited

    Unacceptable risk — banned outright (for example, social scoring or manipulative exploitation).

  2. Tier 2

    High risk

    Strict lifecycle duties: risk management, data quality, documentation, human oversight, and conformity checks (for example, hiring or credit).

  3. Tier 3

    Limited risk

    Transparency duties: disclose when people interact with AI or view synthetic content (for example, customer chatbots).

  4. Tier 4 · Lowest

    Minimal risk

    No extra AI Act obligations beyond existing general law (for example, spam filters or basic recommendations).

On the exam, classify the use case first and match controls to its tier — and reclassify whenever the purpose, audience, or data changes.

Study heuristics

Six decision rules

Unofficial study heuristics distilled for quick scenario triage — not official AWS wording.

  1. 01

    Smallest change first

    Try prompt engineering before RAG, and RAG before fine-tuning or custom training.

  2. 02

    Measure before and after

    Capture a baseline or run a controlled A/B comparison before claiming ROI or savings.

  3. 03

    Owner before scale

    Pair every production rollout with a named accountable owner and run-state operations so pilots do not stall.

  4. 04

    Enable rather than only ban

    Shadow AI persists when staff have a real need and no approved option; pair clear rules with sanctioned tools.

  5. 05

    Human review scales with harm

    Use human-in-the-loop approval for high-harm or irreversible decisions, and human-on-the-loop or confidence escalation for lower-risk flows.

  6. 06

    Data readiness gates the pilot

    High business value paired with broken or missing data is a data-quality project first, not a quick pilot.
